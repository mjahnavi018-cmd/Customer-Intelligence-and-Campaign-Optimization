# Marketing Campaign & Customer Analytics — Final Report
*An end-to-end framework for customer intelligence, campaign response, marketing performance and resource allocation.*

All figures in `outputs/figures/`, all numbers in `outputs/tables/` (regenerated by `python run_pipeline.py`, seed 42).
MU = monetary units of the iFood case. Detailed evidence per finding: `reports/findings.md`.

## 1. Executive summary
A pilot campaign sent to every customer returned 294 responses from 2,019 contacts and **lost 2,823 MU (ROI −47 %)**
because its 14.6 % response rate is far below the 27.3 % break-even implied by the dataset's own economics
(3 MU per contact, 11 MU per response). The data show one dominant, transparent signal: **customers who accepted an
earlier campaign respond 5× more often (40.3 % vs 7.9 %)**. Contacting only those 417 customers would have turned the
loss into **+597 MU** while keeping 57 % of responders. A leakage-controlled machine-learning model, validated on a
*later* campaign than it was trained on, reaches ROC-AUC 0.74 — **not significantly better than that one-line rule**,
so the recommended framework is rule-first, model-second. Customer value is concentrated (top 20 % = 52 % of spend)
but is not the same as responsiveness; purchase-channel mix (catalog vs store) separates value and response but no
channel ROI can be computed. A randomised e-mail experiment (Hillstrom, 64,000 customers) shows that campaigns *cause*
incremental revenue, that the choice of campaign type matters (Mens e-mail +$0.77 vs Womens +$0.42 per customer),
and that per-customer personalisation did not beat sending the single best campaign.

**Decision:** next campaign → 543 P1 customers (≈ 1,629 MU budget), 148 P2 customers only as a randomised test cell,
1,328 P3 customers suppressed; keep a random holdout inside P1 to measure incrementality.

## 2. Business problem
Limited marketing budget; customers differ in value, behaviour, campaign history and channel use. The company must
decide who to target, with which campaign/channel, how much to spend, and why.

## 3. Objective
Combine behaviour, value, campaign response and channel evidence into an evidence-based targeting and
resource-allocation framework — and state how confident each conclusion is.

## 4. Research questions
1. Who are the customers and how concentrated is value?
2. Which campaigns worked, for which segments?
3. Which channels carry volume vs value?
4. Can response to a *future* campaign be predicted better than simple rules?
5. Who should be targeted under a fixed budget, and what is the expected return?
6. Does marketing cause incremental response, and which campaign type should go to whom?
7. Which conclusions are robust?

## 5. Dataset selection
Six public candidates scored on 23 criteria (`DATASET_EVALUATION.md`). **iFood CRM case** (2,240 rows, 6 ordered
campaigns, spend, purchase channels, real per-contact cost / per-response revenue) is the primary dataset;
**Hillstrom** (64,000, randomised e-mail test) is the secondary dataset for causal questions. They are analysed
separately, never merged. Rejected: UCI Bank Marketing, Online Retail II, Criteo Uplift, IBM Marketing CVA.

## 6. Data audit
(`reports/data_audit.md`) 182 exact duplicate registrations (183 after label normalisation) and 19 groups with
conflicting outcomes; one placeholder income; three impossible birth years; seven non-standard marital labels; no
transaction or campaign dates; delivery channel absent; recency nearly uniform; spend window misaligned with
enrolment; Hillstrom randomisation balanced (max |SMD| 0.014). The availability matrix (`DATA_AVAILABILITY.md`)
removes channel ROI, CLV, fatigue, acquisition-channel value and iFood uplift from scope.

## 7. Data preparation
Label normalisation → de-duplication (183 rows) → exclusion of 38 conflicting-label rows → invalid values to NaN +
flags → constants to economics parameters. 2,019 analytical customers; Hillstrom unchanged (typo fix only). Every step
logged in `outputs/tables/cleaning_log.csv`.

## 8. Exploratory analysis
Spend is right-skewed (median 396, max 2,525 MU); wines (50 %) and meat (28 %) dominate revenue. Twelve responder-profile
tests (BH-corrected): all but web visits are significant, but effect sizes range from negligible (Education, V = 0.09)
to moderate (RFM segment, V = 0.27).

## 9. Customer behaviour
Customer table: recency, frequency, spend, AOV, deal share, channel shares and dominant channel, category shares and
entropy, tenure, campaign history. Store is the dominant purchase channel for 1,065 customers; 679 are mixed; only 38
are catalog-dominant.

## 10. RFM analysis
Quintile scores (tie-broken by rank), documented edges (R: 0–19 … 80–99 days; M: 5–55 … 1,173–2,524 MU).
Nine descriptive segments (recency tier × value tier). The pilot response ranges from 1.8 % (Low-value lapsing) to
31.6 % (Core high-value recent); only the core segment would have been profitable on its own (+108 MU). The RFM map
(`fig05`) shows response increasing with both recency and value.

## 11. Customer segmentation
KMeans on eight behavioural features, k chosen by a pre-declared stability/silhouette rule → **k = 2** (silhouette
0.30, ARI 0.98): *High-value full-price* vs *Low-value deal-driven*. It separates response and value **less** than RFM
(V 0.135 vs 0.267; η² 0.60 vs 0.72). **Clustering adds little; RFM remains the segmentation of record.**

## 12. Campaign performance
Acceptance: C1 6.6 %, C2 1.3 %, C3 7.2 %, C4 7.5 %, C5 7.2 %, pilot 14.6 %. Pilot economics (the only campaign with
cost): cost 6,057, revenue 3,234, profit −2,823 MU, ROI −47 %. ROAS/CAC not computable. Funnel observable only as
contacted → accepted.

## 13. Channel analytics
Volume ≠ value: store 46 % of purchases, store-dominant response 6.7 %; catalog 21 % of purchases, highest spend per
user (801 MU), catalog-dominant response 31.6 % (n = 38, wide CI). Web usage intensity rises with response (6.7 % →
20.5 % across terciles). No channel cost/revenue → no channel ROI or budget shift between channels.

## 14. Campaign × segment analysis
C1/C2/C4/C5 over-index 2–3× in high-value segments; C5 reached no low-value customer; C3 is segment-neutral (BH
p = 0.06) and the only campaign over-indexing among low-value recent customers (1.31). Segment differences are
significant for five of six campaigns after BH correction. Campaign fatigue cannot be studied (no exposure histories
or dates).

## 15. Response prediction
Customer × campaign panel; train C2–C5, test the later pilot C6 (no dates exist, but the campaign order does). LOCO
CV for tuning; declared preference for logistic regression unless boosting gains > 0.01 PR-AUC.

| Approach (test C6) | ROC-AUC | PR-AUC | Lift@20 % |
|---|---|---|---|
| B0 overall rate | 0.500 | 0.146 | 1.0 |
| B1 earlier acceptances | 0.728 | 0.396 | 2.79 |
| B2 RFM score | 0.722 | 0.283 | 2.19 |
| **Logistic regression (primary)** | **0.740** | **0.412** | 2.33 |
| Gradient boosting | 0.766 | 0.408 | 2.57 |

Paired bootstrap LR − B1: PR-AUC +0.017 (CI −0.013 to +0.043) → **not significant**. Drivers: earlier acceptances,
spend, income, gold/wine/catalog shares (+); category breadth, meat/fish shares, teenagers at home (−). Leakage audit
(`reports/leakage_audit.md`): Recency is a post-pilot artefact; same-campaign CV would report AUC 0.915.

**Predictive vs incremental:** the iFood data are observational. *The observational dataset supports response
prediction but does not reliably identify incremental treatment effects.*

## 16. Customer value
Historical 2-year spend only (no CLV, no predicted future value). Top 10 % = 30 %, top 20 % = 52 % of spend; 38 % of
customers produce 80 %. High-value tercile response 24.8 % vs 7.0 %. Enrolment cohorts cannot be compared on value
(spend window artefact); acquisition channel is absent.

## 17. Targeting engine
Per customer: RFM segment, cluster, value tier and percentile, recency, campaign history (C1…C6), next-campaign
probability (LR trained on C2–C6, prior-shifted to the pilot rate — assumption), expected profit, purchase-channel
evidence with CIs, priority tier, action, and exact log-odds drivers. Five prioritisation methods were back-tested;
the rule-first hybrid won. Tiers (back-test response on the pilot): **P1** 417 customers, 40.3 % (CI 35.7–45.1 %),
+597 MU; **P2** 161, 14.9 %, −219 MU; **P3** 1,441, 7.1 %, −3,201 MU. Five data-selected case studies
(`outputs/tables/case_studies.md`) include a model-vs-rule disagreement (a never-responding, catalog-heavy,
high-income customer with p = 0.53 → P2 test cell).

## 18. Marketing optimisation
Rank-and-cut (optimal for one channel with a constant contact cost), back-tested on actual outcomes:

| Scenario | Contacts | Responses | Profit (MU) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A Mass contact (actual pilot) | 2,019 | 294 | −2,823 |
| B Historical-responder rule | 417 | 168 | **+597** |
| C RFM top 20 % | 404 | 135 | +273 |
| D Value top 20 % | 404 | 136 | +284 |
| E Model top 20 % | 404 | 137 | +295 |
| F Model raw-probability break-even | 140 | 81 | +471 |

Marginal returns diminish sharply (observed, not assumed): only the first ~15 % of ranked customers pay back 3 MU.
Hindsight-optimal share 5–30 % across cost 2–4 × revenue 8–14 → **sensitive**. Hillstrom: best single e-mail
(+$0.888 per customer, CI 0.53–1.28) = uplift-model policy (+$0.888); e-mail costs are scenario inputs only.

## 19. Baseline comparison
Progressively stronger approaches: overall rate → RFM → earlier acceptances → logistic regression → boosting.
The big step is from "no targeting" to "campaign history" (PR-AUC 0.146 → 0.396); ML adds +0.016 (not significant);
boosting adds nothing on PR-AUC. **Complexity was not earned by the data.**

## 20. Statistical validation
Wilson CIs for all rates; χ²/Cramér's V; Mann–Whitney with rank-biserial; Welch CIs; BH correction for exploratory
families (responder profiles 12 tests, campaign × segment 6, Hillstrom subgroups 88, heterogeneity 24) and Holm for the
9 confirmatory Hillstrom effects; paired bootstrap for model comparisons; customer bootstrap for profits; IPW with
bootstrap for policy values; Qini against 100 random rankings. SQL results cross-checked against Python (25/25 checks).

## 21. Robustness
**Stable:** campaign history is the strongest predictor; model beats random on a later campaign; top 10–20 % targeting
is profitable in the back-test; high-value tier membership across binnings (Jaccard ≥ 0.83); leakage-prone features do
not drive the temporal model; Mens > Womens e-mail. **Sensitive / not confirmed:** model > rule; exact optimal
contact share; mass contact always losing (fails at cost 2 / revenue 14); exact 3×3 RFM membership (ARI 0.70–0.83);
KMeans structure across k; pilot recency effect; model performance across campaigns (AUC 0.65–0.88); subgroup-specific
e-mail effects. De-duplication does not change conclusions (rule AUC 0.723 raw vs 0.728 clean).

## 22. Key findings
1. Campaign history beats everything else; ML does not significantly improve on it.
2. The pilot lost money because it contacted everyone; a history-based fifth of the base was profitable.
3. Naïve same-campaign validation overstates accuracy (0.91 vs 0.74) because of a snapshot-timing artefact.
4. Value is concentrated but not identical to responsiveness — a real volume-vs-quality trade-off.
5. Offer content decides who responds; most campaigns only reach affluent customers.
6. Store is the volume channel, catalog the value channel (association only).
7. E-mails cause incremental revenue; the best single campaign beats personalised assignment at this scale.

## 24. Limitations
Observational iFood data (no causal claims); undocumented provenance, possible synthetic elements and duplicates;
no campaign/transaction dates (no fatigue, drift or CLV); no delivery channel or channel cost (no channel ROI);
cost/revenue known only for the pilot and revenue is not margin; Hillstrom is a different company and era; forward
probabilities rely on a base-rate assumption; the Streamlit dashboard was smoke-tested with a stub in the build
environment (Streamlit could not be installed there) — see README for the testing status.

## 25. Future work
Randomised holdouts in the next campaign (measure incrementality on iFood customers); record campaign dates,
delivery channels and costs to enable channel ROI and fatigue analysis; transaction-level data for BG/NBD-style CLV;
re-test uplift modelling on larger samples or frequent outcomes.

## 26. Conclusion
The most valuable analytics here is not the most complex: an honest audit, a leakage-aware temporal test and a
back-test with the dataset's real economics show that **who to target** is answered by campaign history, **how much to
spend** by the break-even response rate (about a fifth to a quarter of the base), **which campaign** by randomised testing, and
**how confident we are** by explicit robustness classification. Machine learning, clustering and uplift modelling were
all evaluated — and each was kept only to the extent the data earned it.
